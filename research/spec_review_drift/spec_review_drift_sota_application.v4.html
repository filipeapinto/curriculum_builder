01 · Case orientation

02 · Original problem frame

The series began with a reader outcome

Version 1 describes “model-agnostic documentation skills for graph-based LLM systems.” Its desired result is an engaging, accurate architecture and operations guide whose reader can understand how a system is built, deployed, secured, observed, and operated without first reading source specifications.

INPUT

Evidence about an existing graph-based LLM system

Architecture, prompts, schemas, code, configuration, policies, traces, tests, deployment records, and operational evidence.

→
DESIRED CAPABILITY

Documentation creation

An AI-assisted process selects verified facts, organizes them for readers, creates useful views, and labels uncertainty.

→
OUTPUT

Reader-facing system guide

Architecture, deployment, security, contracts, configuration, observation, operation, failure, and recovery.

Contextual point: v1 is already more than a vague idea—it contains quality and content requirements. But its center of gravity is the reader-facing result, not yet the exact repository execution architecture later encoded by v11.
03 · How the idea evolved

From outcome standard to executable skill system

v1
15 Aug
Model-agnostic outcome standard

Eight content/quality sections describe the documentation the reader should receive. Source specifications remain authoritative.

v2
A complementary skill family appears

The series now complements a general graph-doc-* production graph. It introduces staged roles such as goal definition and evidence inventory, structured artifacts, content addressing, stable IDs, and pipeline traceability.

v3–v4
Standalone interoperability and run conformance

The family becomes independently conformant, with adapter rules, required configuration, schemas, terminal states, and conformance procedures.

v5
16 Aug
The design collapses into three skills

Plan, create, and update become the explicit architecture. The plan skill absorbs goal-definition behavior; human plan approval precedes creation or update.

v6–v9
Repository implementation hardens

Schemas and fixtures evolve; provenance, repair, publication, verification, and rendering requirements become more concrete. Git records v7–v9 and the graph visualization skill in the 16 August commit.

v10–v11
17 Aug
Current contract stabilizes

v11 supersedes v10, points to schema v8 and v11 fixtures, clarifies visual-review state, and is committed as the current specification on 17 August.

Why the version history matters: the major conceptual change is not “v10 became v11.” It is that a reader-outcome specification became a repository-specific three-skill product. The available versions show the transformation, but not a complete record of the owner conversations and model-to-model reviews that authorized each step.

05 · Why this became a research trigger

The owner’s concern appears after the system has become implementation-ready

At the point where v11 could be executed, the owner reports that repeated Codex/Claude review has outpaced their ability to retain a complete mental model. That creates three related but distinct questions.

1

Comprehension

Can the owner currently explain the product that v11 will create, including its architecture and recurring operational obligations?

2

Authorization

Were the transition to three skills, local renderer dependency, schemas, fixtures, and approval lifecycle explicitly intended—or progressively normalized?

3

Economics

Is confidence in the desired product high enough to justify the implementation cost, rather than first purchasing cheaper information?

This is why SOTA is relevant: not to prove that v11 drifted, but to determine how much confidence reciprocal LLM review should create, which failure mechanisms are plausible, and which existing controls can discriminate intent preservation before execution.
06 · Applying SOTA to this history

General research mapped to the actual case

SOTA findingCase evidenceWhat followsWhat does not follow
Repeated model transformation can correct or drift; it has no inherent fidelity guarantee.Eleven versions were iteratively reviewed, and the artifact’s product shape changed materially between v1 and v5.Version count and reciprocal review are not sufficient evidence that the endpoint preserves initial intent.It does not prove any particular change was wrong.
Agreement among related agents may reflect correlated priors, conformity, or evaluator lineage.Codex and Claude reviewed one another’s evolving artifacts and therefore shared the same accumulated framing.Later reviewers may have validated internal coherence inside a premise inherited from earlier rounds.It does not mean the two systems made identical errors or that cross-review had no value.
Endogenous evals validate the objective they encode.Schemas and conformance fixtures evolved alongside the specification.Passing v11 fixtures demonstrates contract conformance, not independent preservation of the original desired product.It does not make the fixtures invalid; they are strong downstream controls.
Human approval fails when the decision surface exceeds human comprehension.v11 requires human approvals for its generated artifacts, while the owner reports no longer grasping the governing specification itself.Approval inside the workflow does not resolve authorization of the workflow architecture.It does not mean human approval is useless once the decision surface is restored.
Existing solutions work in layers.The repository already has versioning, schemas, fixtures, digests, stopping rules, and human gates.The missing layer is a protected external comparison between owner-recognized intent and the current product architecture.The solution is not necessarily more process or more reviewers.
07 · Case reasoning

Why full execution is not yet justified

  1. The initiative originally centers on producing a trustworthy reader-facing guide for graph-based LLM systems.
  2. Across v1–v5, that outcome becomes a specific three-skill repository product; later versions harden its artifacts, dependencies, rendering, and publication lifecycle.
  3. v11 is the active endpoint, so executing now means implementing that full product—not merely the original documentation outcome.
  4. The owner reports that the iterative process has broken their ability to recognize and confidently authorize the endpoint.
  5. SOTA establishes that reciprocal LLM refinement can improve or drift and that internal agreement/evals do not independently prove fidelity to an external human objective.
  6. The repository already has strong controls after v11 is accepted; adding more downstream rigor will not answer whether the chosen product architecture should be accepted.
  7. A compact, blind comparison of original outcome, current owner intent, and v11 consequences is cheaper and more informative than full implementation.
Bounded conclusion: the evidence supports pausing full execution. It does not support discarding v11 or declaring the iterative work a failure.

09 · Final statement

v11 now has a place in the story

v11 is the current implementation-ready endpoint of a short, intensive evolution from a model-agnostic documentation outcome into a repository-specific three-skill system. It is reviewed because implementing it would commit the project to that accumulated architecture. The owner’s loss of comprehension makes authorization uncertain; SOTA explains why reciprocal AI review and endogenous conformance cannot settle that uncertainty. The appropriate response is therefore a targeted comparison between original outcome, present owner intent, and concrete v11 consequences—not immediate execution and not automatic rejection.
